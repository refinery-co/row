# 2026 (Sangiovese + Cabernet Sauvignon, Oak Barrel Winecraft, Berkeley)

## THE HEADLINE: this was a potassium vintage, and the fruit arrived that way

**Both musts came in at pH 4.10 on 08-27, before cold soak, before fermentation, before any cellar work.** A red must at 4.1 is a high-potassium must walking in the door.

**The Sangiovese carries the diagnostic tell: Brix 21 at pH 4.1 to 4.3.** That pairing is the signature. Under-ripe fruit is supposed to have *low* pH and *high* acid, because that is what under-ripe means. Low sugar together with high pH says the vine was loading potassium into the skins independent of ripening. That is canopy vigour, irrigation, or site. It is not hang time, and no picking decision fixes it.

Confirming evidence: **Sangiovese TA measured 6.19 g/L at pH 4.03, Cabernet 6.00 g/L at pH 3.90.** Both mid-band for a finished red (Boulton 6.0 to 7.5). Neither wine was ever acid-deficient. They were pH-deficient at a normal acid level, which is the definition of a potassium problem.

### Grapes versus maceration: how the blame splits

| | Must pH | Tartaric pre-ferment | pH at pitch | pH at press | Rebound | Net vs must |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cabernet | 4.10 | 61 g (~2.2 g/L) | 3.50 | 3.90 | +0.40 | −0.20 |
| Sangiovese | 4.30 | 45 g (~1.6 g/L) | 3.30 | 4.03 | +0.73 | −0.27 |

The fruit set the floor. Maceration widened the gap. 106 g of tartaric went in before fermentation and bought a net 0.20 and 0.27 against the original must; the rest was consumed by potassium coming out of the skins.

**The cold soak itself is the least of it.** Potassium extraction is largely alcohol-driven. Cold soak is water-based extraction and preferentially pulls anthocyanins; three days at 47 to 50F does comparatively little on the potassium side. The damage happened during fermentation, with punchdowns 2 to 3 times daily on skins that were already loaded.

### What it actually cost

Correctable, but expensive. Post-press the Sangiovese needed 2.15 g/L of tartaric to move from 4.03 to 3.75, and the Cabernet 2.36 g/L to move 3.90 to 3.61. Both landed inside or adjacent to target, but only by spending nearly the whole TA budget: both finished near TA 8.3, above the Boulton band, relying on MLF to bring them back inside it.

The binding constraint on a potassium wine is **TA, not pH.** Every gram of tartaric adds a full gram-equivalent to TA while moving pH only as far as the buffer allows. On the Sangiovese, going from 3.75 to 3.60 would cost another 28 g and put TA at 9.49, over the TTB 9.0 fixed-acidity line. That is the wall, and it arrives long before the pH target does.

Cold stabilisation is not a rescue either: above pH 3.65, KHT precipitation *raises* pH (AWRI; Zoecklein and Fugelsang, Virginia Tech). It only becomes a tool once a wine is already below 3.65.

### Next harvest, in priority order

1. **Get a must analysis before committing to the fruit.** pH and TA, not just Brix. A red must above pH 4.0 tells you the vintage's ceiling before you have spent a dollar.
2. **On high-pH fruit, shorten the maceration.** Press earlier, punch down less aggressively. Trade extraction for a workable pH. This year's Sangiovese was extracted from 3.30 at pitch back up to 4.03 at press.
3. **Do not spend the acid budget pre-ferment on high-potassium fruit.** Correct only as far as fermentation health requires, then do the real work post-press when the number holds still.
4. **Watch for the Brix-pH mismatch at intake.** Low Brix with high pH is the early warning. Plan a short maceration from day one.

## THE BIG PROCESS LESSON: bench trials are accurate, carboys are slow

**Trust the bench curve. Do not trust a pH reading taken 30 minutes after an addition.**

| | Bench predicted | Read at 30 min | Read next morning | Bench error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sangiovese | 3.78 | 3.93 | **3.75** | −0.03 |
| Cabernet | 3.57 | 3.83 | **3.61** | +0.04 |

Pambianchi's trial predicted both wines within 0.04 pH. The 30-minute readings were off by 0.15 and 0.22 in the same direction, on two different wines with two different doses, and produced a completely false picture: an apparent "40 to 48 percent bulk response," a suspected electrode slope fault, and a conclusion that the Sangiovese was uncorrectable. All three were artifacts of reading an unhomogenised carboy.

**True bulk response, from equilibrated readings: 0.130 pH per g/L on the Sangiovese, 0.123 on the Cabernet.** Almost exactly double what the 30-minute numbers implied.

**Next harvest:**

- Tartaric dissolved in 250 mL and gently stirred into 6.5 gallons needs **hours, not minutes**, to homogenise. Give it overnight before believing any reading.
- Do not chase a number the same night you dose. It will read high, you will over-correct, and the wine cannot be un-acidified.
- Recalibrating the electrode mid-session moved readings *up* 0.03 to 0.04, which added noise to an already-bad signal. Calibrate at the **start** of a session, not when something looks wrong.
- Duplicate every reading. Both TAs and the final Sangiovese pH were run twice and agreed to within 0.02, which is what made them trustworthy.

## Varietal buffering differs, and TA does not explain it

Bench trial curves, pH drop per 0.5 g/L tartaric on a 100 mL sample:

- **Cabernet:** 0.07, 0.07, 0.07, 0.07, 0.07. Perfectly linear.
- **Sangiovese:** 0.06, 0.07, 0.07, 0.04, 0.03, 0.07, 0.03. Flattens through the middle.

**The flat shoulder was initially read as evidence of higher TA. The measurement refuted that:** Sangiovese TA 6.19 versus Cabernet 6.00, a 3 percent difference, against a roughly 40 percent difference in bench response. Nearly identical acid content, very different buffering.

**Buffer capacity and TA are not the same quantity.** What differs between these two wines is potassium, which sets buffer capacity largely independent of how much acid is present. A flat bench curve is a potassium signal, not a TA signal.

**Next harvest:** budget more tartaric for Sangiovese than Cabernet on equal volumes, and read a flattening curve as a warning about the fruit rather than about the acid.

## The landing equation, which is what actually sizes an addition

**Post-MLF TA = starting TA + tartaric added − (0.56 × malic)**

Rearranged: **addition = target TA − starting TA + (0.56 × malic)**

Requires TA and malic measured, not pH. Target band 6.0 to 7.5 g/L for reds (Boulton 1999), high-acid varieties in the upper half. Adding 1 g/L of tartaric raises TA by exactly 1 g/L, by construction: tartaric is diprotic, MW 150.09, TA equivalent weight 75.04. Full sourcing in `Wine Knowledge Base/Acid Management and MLF - Sourced Reference.md`.

## Mistakes to not repeat

1. **Nitrogen went in late on both wines.** Fermaid O at 57 percent sugar depletion on the Cabernet and 68 percent on the Sangiovese. The window is the first third. Set an alarm at pitch. On the Cabernet this also triggered a late DAP addition as mitigation, which is a patch, not a plan.
2. **Both pitched at 60F, below the 65 to 75F ideal.** The Cabernet went through an extended lag phase. Warm the must to 65F before pitching, or belt it.
3. **Free run and press fraction were not separated on the Cabernet.** Pressed to a 6 gal bucket plus a 1 gal jug without a clean split. Decide the split before pressing starts.
4. **TA was not measured until 09-08**, after every acid decision had already been made on pH alone. pH measures acid strength, TA measures amount, and the palate reads amount. Titrate at every checkpoint.
5. **A bucket reading over settling lees read about 0.08 pH higher than the racked wine.** Take official readings after racking.
6. **Sanitizer on the probe produced a false 3.4 reading** during the Sangiovese cold soak corrections. Rinse with distilled water between every reading.
7. **A carboy was bulk-degassed by mistake** while preparing a TA sample. Degassing is a 50 mL sample operation, never a carboy operation. It stripped the CO2 blanket off a wine carrying zero SO2 and exposed it to oxygen. It also changed nothing: TA read 6.20 before and 6.18 after, so there was no CO2 inflation to recover.
8. **Volume drifted between the log and the bench trial workbook.** The Sangiovese sheet carried 6.00 gal against a true 6.5 gal, which would have under-dosed by 6.5 g. Record true volume at every rack and update every downstream sheet in the same sitting.
9. **Three hours of analysis were built on unequilibrated pH readings.** See the process lesson above. This was the single largest waste of the vintage and it nearly caused a bad decision.

## Equipment notes

- Vinmetrica SC-300 and Apera PH20 agreed closely on the main lots (4.11 vs 4.10) but diverged on the field blend (4.3 vs 4.0). Calibrate both before harvest and treat a large divergence as a signal to recalibrate, not to average.
- **Malic reagents froze in storage, and that was correct.** The SC-50 / SC-55 manual says "Keep in Freezer." Vinmetrica ships the kit frozen. Thaw to room temperature, mix well, use within about 14 days. An earlier note in this file suspected denaturation; that concern applies to refrigerate-only enzymatic kits, not this one. Retracted.
- **The SC-55 has a low ceiling and a pre-MLF baseline sits above it.** The manual cautions against exceeding about 1.4 g/L in the vial. Both 2026 baselines ran over it (Cabernet 1.48, Sangiovese 1.62 in-vial), so both results are conservative and true malic is likely higher. Dilute the boiled and restored sample 1:1 with distilled water before the diluent step next time and multiply by 4 instead of 2.
- The Pambianchi calculator suite (© Daniel Pambianchi, 2021) is the working toolset and it earned its keep this vintage: the TA-pH trial predicted both wines within 0.04 pH. The tool uses only the **measured TA** field from STEP 1, to predict final TA. Leaving it blank silently disables that check.

## Decisions made this vintage, for reference

- No SO2 at all pre-fermentation. Low-intervention approach with IOC Gaia (Biodiva, Torulaspora delbrueckii) for spoilage suppression instead.
- Yeasts: BDX for the Cabernet, BM 4X4 for the Sangiovese (BM45 blend, 16 percent alcohol tolerance).
- MLF with Viniflora Oenos 2.0, direct inoculation, chosen for zero biogenic amine production. Opti'Malo Plus at the label rate of 20 g/hL, which is 4.9 g per 6.5 gal carboy, not the 1 g/gal originally written in the plan.
- Oak split for comparison: French cubes in one carboy, American in the other.
- Field blend press fraction used as topping wine rather than kept as a separate batch, which is why both carboys read 6.5 gal post-rack.

## Where it ended up

Measured after overnight equilibration, calibrated electrode, Sangiovese duplicated:

Final, after malic was measured and the acid work closed on both wines.

| | Total tartaric post-press | Malic | TA pre-MLF | pH pre-MLF | Post-MLF TA | Post-MLF pH |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cabernet | 58 g (2.36 g/L) | 2.96 | 8.36 | **3.61** | 6.70 | ~3.76 |
| Sangiovese | **83 g (3.373 g/L)** | 3.23 | **9.56** | ~3.59 | **7.75** | **~3.74** |

Both bench decisions were vindicated. The Cabernet finished within 4 g of its original trial number and hit 3.61 against a 3.60 target. The Sangiovese took a third tranche of 30 g after the competition research below reversed the direction of the recommendation.

**Cabernet:** acid work held open by design. It projects to pH 3.76 post-MLF. If the measured post-MLF pH lands at 3.65 or below, cold stabilization begins lowering pH instead of raising it, which is worth having. Forcing that now would take about 22 g and finish at TA 7.60, but it would be a bet against a plus or minus 0.05 estimate of the MLF pH rise. Measure, then decide.

**Sangiovese handling from here:** free SO2 near 52 ppm after MLF at pH 3.74, against the 74 ppm that pH 3.90 would have required. That difference is the entire justification for the third tranche.

## The competition frame reversed the acid recommendation

The question was whether aiming at an award wine rather than a technically sound one changes the acid call. It does, and it argues for **more** tartaric, for reasons that have nothing to do with hitting benchmark numbers.

**You cannot acidify to award numbers.** Top-typicality Sangiovese measures pH 3.42 / TA 5.18 (Sacchi et al., Chianti Classico, panel score 9.54). Italian average 3.36 / 6.14. California average 3.63 / 5.08. Their TA is LOW, and adding acid moves TA away from that, not toward it.

**But acidity is barely scored, and faults are.** On the Davis 20-point scale total acidity is worth 2 points and "slightly high" costs 1. Aroma/bouquet (4) and general quality (4) are 8 points, and that is where faults land. Judges report seeing oxidation, excess SO2, volatile acidity and lack of balance. High pH drives all four.

**The mechanism is SO2.** AWRI requires 0.6 mg/L molecular SO2 to stop Brettanomyces. That is 74 ppm free at pH 3.90 and 52 ppm at 3.74. At 74 ppm the sulfur is sensorially obvious and becomes the fault. At 52 ppm nobody notices.

**And colour.** At pH 3.4 only about 10 percent of monomeric anthocyanins sit in the coloured flavylium form, and the fraction falls as pH rises. Lower pH makes the wine visibly brighter, which is 4 more Davis points sitting in clarity and colour.

The acid buys fault-avoidance, not points. That is the whole argument, and it is why the Sangiovese went from 20 g to 30 g.

Full write-up with sources: `Wine Knowledge Base/Competition Targets - Acid and pH for Award Wines.md`.

## The fruit was diagnosed before it was bought, and the diagnosis was published

Oak Barrel publishes Brix, pH and TA for every lot in August, before ordering. The 2026 sheet listed the Sangiovese at **pH 4.16** with the vendor's own note "needs tartaric acid to reach 7.5 TA," and the Cabernet at **pH 3.94** with the same caution. Of the six reds offered, **Nebbiolo from Tehama arrived at pH 3.75 / TA 7.2 and needed nothing.**

The "get a must analysis before committing to the fruit" lesson at the top of this notebook is therefore already solved. The analysis exists, it is free, and it is on the order page.

Two further findings:

**Pre-ferment tartaric on this fruit was almost entirely consumed.** Both wines were adjusted toward the vendor's 7.5 TA target before fermentation and both came back to roughly their starting TA by pressing. The Sangiovese went in at 6.0, had 45 g added, and measured 6.19 at press. Roughly 1.4 g/L of added acid disappeared from each wine. Correct only as far as the ferment requires; spend the budget post-press.

**The delivered fruit was worse than the sheet.** Sangiovese arrived 2 Brix under the published 23, which forced the chaptalization. Cabernet read pH 4.10 against a published 3.94.

Full write-up: `Wine Knowledge Base/Grape Sourcing - Oak Barrel 2026 Lot Data and Site Notes.md`.

## Two corrections to earlier entries in this notebook

**1. The 9.0 g/L TTB line was being applied to the wrong number.** 27 CFR 24.182 caps acid additions at 9.0 g/L fixed acid **in the finished wine**, not at any transient pre-MLF value. Earlier entries here treated a pre-MLF TA above 9.0 as a wall and sized additions against it. The Sangiovese finished at 9.56 pre-MLF and lands at 7.75 post-MLF, which is compliant with room. Even 36 g would have been legal. This over-constrained several decisions this vintage.

**2. Chaptalization has a downstream cost that nearly went unnoticed.** The Sangiovese went OG 1.105 to FG 1.001, which is 13.65 percent ABV at the x131.25 factor and 14.04 percent at x135. **Viniflora Oenos 2.0 is rated to a maximum of 14.0 percent.** The two Brix of added sugar, put in to cover a shortfall against the vendor's published number, is what moved this MLF from comfortable to marginal. The Cabernet at 12.86 to 13.23 percent has a full point of headroom.

Consequence: the Sangiovese MLF runs at **72 to 77F**, not the 65 to 70F originally written, and gets a malic check at day 7 rather than week 3. A stuck MLF on a zero-SO2 wine is the worst available outcome because it cannot be sulfured until the malic is gone.

**Next harvest: pick the ML strain against projected alcohol before you chaptalize, not after.**
